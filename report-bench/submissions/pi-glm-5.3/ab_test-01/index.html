A/B test read-out  ·  online medical consultation  ·  18,000 sessions

Faster answers, same completions — except in dermatology

A trial of a new consultation-queue design cut the wait for a doctor's first reply by nearly a third and almost eliminated long waits. It did not move the completion rate overall, but it did reshape who drops out, and it hurt one department badly: dermatology lost 4.5 points of completion.

01

The setup

Users of an online consultation service were split into a control arm and a test arm and followed for six weeks. Each row is one consultation: who the user was, which department and complaint, when they entered the queue, whether they were lost before a doctor answered, whether the consultation completed, how long the doctor took to respond, how long the consultation ran, and whether the user came back within seven days.

The two arms are close to equal in size and run over the same 42 days, so arm-to-arm differences are not driven by who was routed where or by the calendar.

02

Speed: the change works, and it works everywhere

Time to a doctor's first reply falls by about 48 seconds on average and by 22 seconds at the median. More importantly, the slow end of the distribution is compressed: the 90th percentile drops from 234 s to 177 s and the 95th from 290 s to 213 s. Waits over five minutes — the ones users notice and abandon over — fall from 4.48% of measured sessions to 1.06%.

The gain is not concentrated in one place

Every department, platform and complaint improves, but by very different amounts. Gynecology shows the largest fall — from 210.4 s to 97.2 s — because its control-arm queue was the slowest. The WeChat mini-program arm falls from 198.2 s to 104.3 s, and the 25 complaint types go from a 12.6 s gain (chest tightness) up to a 315.6 s gain (heavy periods).

03

Completion: flat overall, but the mix moved

Headline completion is unchanged: 7,903 of 8,987 test consultations finish (87.9%) against 7,933 of 9,013 in control (88.0%). Underneath that flat total, users lost before a doctor ever answered rise from 427 (4.74%) to 476 (5.30%) — and that increase is not spread evenly.

Dermatology is the exception to an otherwise benign read-out. Completion there falls from 85.0% (1,376 of 1,618) to 80.5% (1,210 of 1,503), a 4.5-point drop with a two-proportion z of −3.35 — far outside what sampling noise would produce at this sample size.

The mechanism is visible one column over: the share of dermatology users who leave the queue before a doctor answers doubles, from 7.7% to 12.7%. Pediatrics moves the other way, from 3.1% to 2.5%, and its completion rate rises from 88.9% to 90.4%.

The pattern holds across every skin complaint. All five lose more users before the doctor in the test arm:

Dermatology was also the slowest department to complete in control (85.0%) and the fastest to improve its response time (153.0 s → 99.9 s), so faster triage did not translate into more finished skin consultations. Skin complaints are the most visual and the most likely to be ones a user abandons if the first answer feels like a form reply — a plausible but untested explanation, since the data records no reason for the exit.

04

After the consultation: shorter visits, and a follow-up shift

Completed consultations run about a minute and a half shorter in the test arm — 11.7 minutes against 13.1 on average, 10.4 against 11.2 at the median — so the time saved at the front of the queue is not spent back at the end.

The swap is the most useful commercial detail in the read-out: the test converts more first-time users into a second visit, and slightly fewer loyal ones. If the goal is acquisition, that is a win; if lifetime value sits with returning users, it is a trade rather than a gain.

05

Reading the numbers honestly

Five things a reader should know before quoting any figure above.

  1. Two labels needed cleaning. Ten rows read '实验组 ' and nine read '对照组 ' with a trailing space, so an untrimmed value count returns four groups — 9,004, 8,977, 10 and 9 — instead of the two real arms. Every figure here trims the label first.
  2. Response time is missing exactly where it should be. All 903 sessions lost before a doctor answered have no response time; all 17,097 others have one. Averages over response time therefore describe people who got an answer, not everyone who arrived.
  3. Consultation length only exists for completed cases. All 2,164 incomplete consultations are blank, so the length figures describe completed work only.
  1. Effects are reported at face value. The 4.5-point dermatology drop carries a two-proportion z of −3.35 and the schedule-wide completion difference is 0.08 points; only the former is large relative to its standard error. The other department moves are within roughly one point and should be read as directional.
  2. This is an observational split, not a controlled trial. Groups were assigned at entry with no record of the assignment rule in the data, so a department-level difference could reflect routing, staffing or case mix rather than the new queue alone.

What the data supports: the new queue design halves long waits and cuts average response time by about a third without changing overall completion. What it warns: dermatology loses completions in a way that repeats across all five skin complaints, and returning users come back less often. A dermatology-specific fix — or excluding skin cases from the new routing — is the obvious next test.
