A behavioural study · 30-minute video conversations between strangers

Thirty Minutes With a Stranger

People who had never met were paired for half an hour of video conversation. Each of them rated their mood before the call, at the start, in the middle and at the end, then rated the person on the other side of the screen. Across 1,656 conversations the mood climbs from the first minute to the last — and people consistently walk away believing they were liked less than they actually were.

01  ·  The shape of the half hour

A conversation that keeps getting better

The mood ratings trace a very simple shape: a flat, slightly nervous opening, then a steady climb that is still going when the call ends. Averaged over every conversation, positive affect sits at 6.10 before the call, edges up to 6.16 as the conversation opens, reaches 6.93 at the halfway mark and 7.38 at the close.

Arousal — how energised people feel rather than how positive — follows the same ramp from a lower base: 5.32 beforehand, then 5.67, 6.40 and 6.70. People do not merely feel better after talking to a stranger; they wake up during it.

The rise is not driven by a few ecstatic outliers. Of the conversations with both a before and an after rating, 82.6% finish higher than they started, 9.4% finish lower and 8.0% end exactly where they began. The typical conversation gains 1.29 points of affect, with a median gain of 1.50. Individual conversations range from a slide of 4.50 points to a leap of 7.00.

02  ·  Who gains the most

The people who arrive low are the ones who move

Split the conversations by how the pair felt before the call and the pattern is sharp. Pairs who started below 5 on affect gained 2.32 points, climbing from 4.56 to 6.88. Pairs who were already above 7 gained only 0.17, drifting from 7.73 to 7.91. The two ends of the scale converge: whatever mood you bring into the call, you tend to leave it close to a 7.

03  ·  What each person thought of the other

Everyone likes their partner more than they think they are liked

After the call each person answered two symmetric questions: how much do you like this person, and how much do you think this person likes you? On a 1-to-7 scale, people rated their own liking at 5.99 but expected only 5.35 in return. The expectation was wrong: the partner's own answer, averaged over the same pairs, was also 5.99.

So the room is full of mutual warmth that neither side registers. Measured person by person against what the partner actually reported, the gap averages 0.64 points, with a median of 1.00 and a range from −6 to +6 across 3,206 people.

54.9% of people underestimate how much their partner likes them. Only 18.3% overestimate, and 26.8% get it exactly right.

The asymmetry is systematic rather than random noise: if people were merely guessing badly, underestimates and overestimates would be equally common. Instead, the error leans heavily towards assuming less affection than was actually there.

The same caution shows up in how people think they were seen. They rate their partner more intelligent (7.68) and kinder (7.98) than they believe the partner rates them (7.06 and 7.43): they hand out generous judgements and assume a stingier verdict comes back.

04  ·  What actually predicts a good conversation

Shared reality, not shared background

The pairs differed in sex, race, age, education and politics, and the study recorded each difference. None of it predicts much. The strongest correlate of how enjoyable a conversation felt is a demographic difference that reaches only 0.085 in absolute size (education). Politics sits at −0.065, race at −0.050, sex at 0.028.

What does predict it is whether the two people felt they were experiencing the same thing. Shared reality correlates 0.570 with enjoyment and 0.532 with liking the partner — an order of magnitude larger than any background difference. On average pairs rated shared reality 4.11 on a 0-to-6 scale.

The same story survives if you rank conversations by how enjoyable people found them. The least enjoyable fifth (mean enjoyment 5.76) gained just 0.40 points of affect over the call. The most enjoyable fifth (mean 9.00) gained 2.25. Good conversations are not just pleasant — they are the ones that change how people feel.

05  ·  The impression people leave

Kind, polite, trustworthy — and not remotely funny

Partners rated each other on a set of traits on the same 1-to-9 scale, and the profile is lopsided. Warmth traits score high: kind 7.98, polite 7.93, trustworthy 7.51. Humour sits far below at 4.72 — the one thing strangers are reliably willing to say their new acquaintance is not.

That the lows are rated far below the average is its own signal: the worst moment of a conversation averages 5.80, the best 7.83, and the overall impression 7.32. Almost everyone has a dip; almost nobody lets it define the half hour.

06  ·  Two people, one conversation

Same half hour, different reports

Each conversation produces two independent accounts, and they do not match. On average the two partners' affect ratings differ by 1.36 points, their arousal ratings by 1.31 and their shared-reality ratings by 1.14. Whatever a conversation is, it is not experienced the same way twice.

Who does the perceiving matters as well. Lonelier participants expected less liking from their partner — a correlation of −0.141 between loneliness and expected liking — while liking the partner just as much as anyone else (−0.018). The deficit shows up in what they expect to receive, not in what they give.

How these numbers were computed

Two tables: data/convos.csv (one row per conversation, with pair-level averages and differences) and data/people.csv (one row per person per conversation, with that person's own ratings and background).
