Faster first replies, no more finished consultations

The test arm cut the median wait for a doctor's first reply from 110 to 88 seconds — a fifth less waiting, across the whole distribution. Yet completion did not move at all (87.94% versus 88.02%), and neither did 7-day follow-up. The flat average hides two opposite effects: new users came back 3.0 points more often, while dermatology patients gave up before a doctor ever replied 5.1 points more often. One is a reason to ship; the other is a reason to fix the rollout first.

The verdict in four numbers

Read: the change did what it was built to do — it made doctors answer sooner — but answering sooner did not make patients stay. None of the three headline gaps clears a two-proportion z of ±1.96 (completion −0.16, abandonment +1.72, follow-up −0.32), so on the aggregate metrics this test is a null result. 02

The wait really did fall — everywhere in the distribution

Whatever the change was, it worked on its own terms. The median first reply drops from 110.0 s in control to 88.0 s in the test arm, a 20% cut, and it is not a median-only trick: every percentile from the 10th to the 95th moves left by a similar proportion. wait-cut wait-tail

03

And nothing downstream moved

Twenty-two seconds saved per patient, 17,000 times over, and the three metrics that matter commercially did not budge. Completion is 87.94% in the test arm against 88.02% in control. Abandonment before a doctor replies is 5.30% against 4.74% — nominally worse. Seven-day follow-up is 12.15% against 12.31%. completion-flat abandonment-worse followup-flat

The one place the test arm is worse is nominally abandonment — but it is not significant

The +0.56 point abandonment gap gives z = 1.72, short of 1.96. On the pooled sample you would not call it a real effect. It becomes very real once you split by department: almost all of it comes from dermatology (§5), where the same comparison returns z = 4.67. This is the clearest example in the data of an average concealing a segment effect.

04

Why a faster reply could not have moved completion

Once a doctor has replied, the consultation almost always finishes: completion among the 17,097 users who reached a doctor is between 90.5% and 93.1% across every wait bucket from under a minute to ten minutes. Waiting 90 seconds instead of 150 simply does not change what happens next. wait-hardly-moves-completion

The measurement gap that matters

first_response_seconds is blank on exactly the 903 rows flagged lost_before_doctor. The users who actually gave up while waiting have no recorded wait at all — the clock stops when they leave. So this dataset can show that the test arm answers faster for people who stayed, and it can show how many people left, but it cannot link the two. structural-missingness

That is why the abandonment number has to be read as the outcome of interest in its own right, rather than as a consequence of a measured wait. Logging the time-to-abandon for users who leave would make the central question of this experiment answerable.

Consultations also got shorter

Median consultation length is 10.4 minutes in the test arm against 11.2 in control, and the mean is 11.67 vs 13.09 minutes. consult-shorter Part of the mean gap is artefact: six of the seven consultations recorded at exactly 640 minutes are in the control arm, and excluding them the means are 11.59 vs 12.61. consult-outliers The residual shortening is consistent with a faster, more transactional exchange — which sits oddly with a retention goal, and matches what §6 finds for returning users.

05

Where it broke: dermatology patients leave before a doctor replies

Split the arms by department and the null result falls apart. In four of five departments the test arm is better on both completion and abandonment. In 皮肤科 dermatology it is sharply worse: 12.71% of test users are lost before a doctor replies against 7.66% in control, and completion falls from 85.04% to 80.51%. department-split dermatology-harm

It is not one bad complaint

All five dermatology complaints regress, and none of them marginally: the test arm loses 11.7%–13.9% of users before a doctor replies against 6.0%–9.7% in control. dermatology-all-complaints

And it is statistically clear

The dermatology gap is not sampling noise: 191 of 1,503 test users were lost against 124 of 1,618 control users, a two-proportion z of 4.67. dermatology-loss-significant

The rest of the service does benefit

Strip dermatology out and the test arm completes 89.43% of sessions (6,693 of 7,484) against 88.67% in control (6,557 of 7,395) — a +0.76 point lift that the pooled average was cancelling out. excl-dermatology-lift

Dermatology was already the weakest department

Even in control, 皮肤科 has the lowest completion (85.04%), the highest abandonment (7.66%) and the lowest follow-up (8.30%) of the five departments, and its five complaints occupy the bottom five rows of the complaint ladder in §8. It is the department with the least slack, so a routing change that shifts load onto it shows up here first. complaint-spread

06

Where it helped: new users come back, returning users do not

Follow-up is flat in aggregate — 12.15% versus 12.31% — but that average is two opposite effects cancelling. Among 新用户 new users the test arm lifts 7-day follow-up from 6.84% to 9.84% (+3.00 pp, z = 4.1). Among 老用户 returning users it drops from 14.93% to 13.22% (−1.71 pp, z = −2.7). Both clear ±1.96. followup-flips-by-user-type followup-split-significant

A plausible reading

New users are the group whose baseline follow-up is lowest — 8.32% against 14.07% for returning users — so they have the most to gain from a first contact that feels responsive. user-type-baseline Returning users, who already have a relationship with the service, instead get a measurably shorter consultation (median 10.4 vs 11.2 minutes overall, §4) and come back slightly less often.

Completion does not show this split — new users complete 88.52% in test versus 88.12% in control and returning users 87.67% versus 87.97% — so the effect is on return behaviour, not on finishing the visit. That is a retention story, and retention is where the money is.

Actionable

If the change ships only to new users, it captures the +3.00 pp follow-up gain with no dermatology exposure to speak of in that segment, and it avoids the returning-user regression entirely. That is a smaller, safer rollout than the one tested here.

07

Demand grew 47% mid-test, and only control buckled

Volume was not stable across the six weeks: sessions per week climb from 2,409 to 3,546, a 47.2% increase. volume-growth From the week of 9 December the control arm's median wait jumps from roughly 85 s to 130–134 s and stays there, while the test arm moves from roughly 77 s to 95–96 s and holds. wait-under-load

Why this matters for the read-out

Because the first three weeks and the last three weeks are effectively different experiments (light load vs heavy load), the pooled comparison in §3 mixes two regimes. Control's pooled median of 110.0 s is a blend of ~85 s weeks and ~132 s weeks; the test arm's 88.0 s is a blend of ~78 s and ~95 s. The gap between arms widened over the test, which means the 20% headline understates the benefit under the load the service is actually running at now.

08

The complaint ladder: a 10-point spread the experiment never touched

Completion varies from 81.0% for 手臂上起红疹,特别痒 (arm rash, 612 users) to 91.1% for 鼻子不通,黄鼻涕多 (blocked nose, 403) and 经期量突然变多 (heavy periods, 505). complaint-spread That 10-point spread is far larger than anything the experiment produced, and it is almost entirely a department effect: the five worst complaints are the five dermatology ones.

Data quality: nine things to fix before the next read-out

The file is usable, but it needs cleaning before it can be trusted, and two of its gaps are structural rather than accidental. Every figure on this page is computed after trimming the padded group labels and normalising the three slash-format dates; without that, the arms would split into four groups and the window into 45 days. padded-group-labels slash-dates

FieldIssueDetailRows
The one that changes the analysis

Wait time is unobservable exactly where it matters. The 903 users who abandoned have no first_response_seconds value, so the causal question this experiment was designed to answer — "does a longer wait make patients leave?" — cannot be asked of this table. Everything in §2 describes the waits of people who stayed. Logging time-to-abandon would fix it. structural-missingness

10

What we would do next

  1. Do not ship the change as tested.

    The pooled read is a null on completion (z = −0.16) with a nominal regression in abandonment (z = +1.72) and a clear, significant regression in one department. Shipping as-is trades a real dermatology loss for an unmeasured aggregate gain.

  2. Fix dermatology routing before anything else.

    Find out why the test arm loses 12.71% of dermatology users before a doctor replies against 7.66% in control. The most likely mechanism is that a faster-answer policy pulls dermatology doctors onto higher-volume queues, leaving that queue thin. All five dermatology complaints regress, so this is a capacity/routing problem, not a specific-symptom one.

  3. Re-run scoped to new users.

    New users gain +3.00 pp on 7-day follow-up (z = 4.1) — the only unambiguous win in the data — while returning users lose 1.71 pp (z = −2.7). A new-user-only rollout captures the gain and sidesteps the loss. Measure follow-up at 30 days as well; seven days is a short horizon for a retention claim.

  4. Stop reporting raw means for wait time.

    Control's mean of 152.0 s is inflated by a 19.8-hour outlier; the median (110.0 s) and the trimmed mean (129.6 s) tell the same story and are not hostage to nine rows. Report p50/p90/p95 as the standard set — they are already computed here.

  5. Instrument abandonment.

    Record time-to-abandon and the queue state at the moment a user leaves. Without it, the experiment's core hypothesis is untestable and the dermatology regression can only be observed, not explained.

  6. Clean the pipeline at the source.

    Trim group on write, enforce ISO dates in enter_date, and investigate the 640-minute cap on consult_minutes and the nine >900-second waits — the cap in particular suggests a session-timeout being written as a consultation length.

  7. Re-read the test against current load.

    Traffic grew 47% during the run and the arms diverged after week 4. Analyse the heavy-load weeks separately: the benefit under today's traffic is likely larger than the pooled 20% suggests, and the dermatology harm should be checked for the same interaction.
