The Pocket Gap

Front and back pocket measurements of men's and women's jeans from popular brands. Across the 80 pairs in this file, men's front pockets average 23.13 in maximum height against 14.33 for women's — a ratio of 1.61. Back pockets, pocket widths and prices sit close to parity. The gap is deep, front-only, and it does not shrink as the price goes up.

What was measured

Each row is one pair of jeans: a brand, a product name, a fit label, a fabric string, a price, and nine pocket measurements — maximum and minimum height and width for the front pocket and the back pocket, plus the height of the front rivet.

A balanced sample of 20 brands

80 jeans · 40 men's, 40 women's · exactly two of each per brand

Every one of the 20 brands contributes two men's jeans and two women's jeans, so the comparisons below are never driven by an uneven sample. Fit labels differ by side: women's jeans are labelled skinny (20), straight (16) or boot-cut (4); men's are labelled skinny (12), straight (12), slim (8), boot-cut (7) or regular (1).

The file records no units. Garment specs of this kind are normally centimetres, and the numbers behave like centimetres (a 23 × 16 front pocket on men's jeans, a 14 × 15 one on women's), so this page reads them as cm — but every figure quoted is exactly the value in the file.

Where the gap is — and where it isn't

Divide each men's mean by the matching women's mean and the pattern is unmistakable: the gap is a front-pocket, vertical phenomenon. Widths are near parity, and back pockets are near parity.

Women's back pockets are only slightly smaller — 15.12 vs 16.01 in maximum height. The front rivet, by contrast, sits higher on men's jeans on average, 7.06 vs 6.25, and the men's rivet range is wider (4.5–9.2 against 4.5–7.5).

Read together: the visible, decorative back pocket is treated much the same on both sides of the gender split. The pocket you actually put things in is not.

Money does not buy pocket

Mean price is effectively identical — $81.18 for men's jeans, $80.32 for women's. Within each side, the correlation between price and front pocket height is slightly negative: -0.262 for men's, -0.151 for women's.

The means line up almost exactly. The medians go the other way — women's jeans in this sample are more expensive in the middle of the range (78.75 vs 69.72), while the men's median is pulled down by budget workwear brands.

Both extremes of the price range belong to women's jeans, and neither extreme is a deep pocket: the $9.99 H&M pair measures 19.0, the $249.0 True Religion pair 14.5.
06

The fit label tells you nothing

If pockets followed the fit label, a boot-cut or straight jean would carry more room than a skinny one. They don't. Within each side the style means are almost flat, and the two sides never meet.

Six of the eight "boot"-named straight jeans come from brands whose men's equivalents are labelled boot-cut. Fit labels in this file are best read as marketing categories, not measurements — which is why every comparison on this page uses the measured numbers instead.

Cutout jeans cost far more on average — 133.10 against 74.11 — and they are mostly men's (6 of the 9). They come from four brands only: True Religion (4), Guess (2), J. Crew (2) and Uniqlo (1).

The file does not define what "cutout" means, so this page treats it as a flag rather than a feature. It is the one column that separates strongly on price.
08

Reading this file with care

Four things a reader should know before quoting these numbers.

  • Brand spellings are inconsistent. The brand column holds 22 distinct raw strings that reduce to 20 brands once case and surrounding whitespace are normalised — "Guess " with a trailing space, and "7 for all Mankind" beside "7 for All Mankind". Everything here uses the normalised form.
  • "min" is not always smaller than "max". In 14 rows the recorded minHeightFront is larger than maxHeightFront — 12 of them women's jeans, 2 men's. The two columns are therefore not the two ends of one measurement, and this report quotes them separately rather than averaging or differencing them.
  • Fit labels drift from product names. Six women's jeans labelled "straight" have "boot" in the name, including Levi's Classic Bootcut.
  • No units, one size, one sample. The file states no unit, records no waist size, and gives one measurement per product rather than a range across sizes — so nothing here speaks to how pockets scale with size, and nothing measures a pocket after wear.
