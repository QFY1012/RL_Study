A data report on 1,656 paired video conversations

Two strangers, thirty minutes, and a surprising amount of misreading.

Strangers were paired for half-hour video conversations and asked, at several points, how they felt and what they thought of each other. Almost everyone left in a better mood than they arrived. Almost nobody could say how their partner felt — and nearly everybody under-estimated how much they were liked.

01 — What the data is

Two tables, one conversation each

Every conversation contributes two rows to people.csv — one per participant, with their own ratings at each point of the call — and one row to convos.csv, which averages the pair and records how far apart they were. Between them they describe 1,656 half-hour video conversations among 1,456 people, some of whom took part more than once.

Three different scales are mixed in the same file
Minimum and maximum of every rating item, people.csv

Completeness: 55 people never finished the questionnaire

Both pre_affect and pre_arousal are present for all 3,312 rows — everyone was asked how they felt before the call. But 55 rows are missing every post-conversation rating (begin, middle, end, overall, worst affect, arousal, enjoyment and both liking items), and 56 rows lack shared_reality and the demographics. Those 55 people started the conversation and left before answering how it went; the analysis of change over time therefore rests on 3,257 people with both a beginning and an end rating.

One quirk worth flagging. The politics column only ever takes the values 1, 2, 3, 4 and 6 — not a single participant is coded 5. The largest groups are at 4 (36.7%) and 6 (27.8%). Whatever the intended scale, it has a hole in it, so political distance is treated below as a raw gap between two codes, not as an ideological measurement.
02 — Ratings through the conversation

Mood climbs the whole way

People were asked how they felt before the call, at its beginning, in the middle, at the end, and for the conversation overall. The answer moves in one direction: from 6.10 before the call to 7.38 at the end, with arousal rising alongside it from 5.32 to 6.70.

Conversations are not flat. The pair's best moment averaged 7.83 and its worst 5.80; even the worst moment across all pairs only bottoms out at 1.0, and the best reaches 9.0.

The gain depends entirely on where you started

The average of +1.23 hides the structure. Sort people by how they felt at the beginning of the call and the pattern inverts: those who started miserable gained enormously, those who started wonderful lost a little. Someone starting at 1 rose by 4.00 points; someone starting at 5 rose 1.93; someone starting at 8 rose only 0.20; and the people who started at the ceiling of 9 came back down by 0.21. Part of this is plain regression to the mean, but the size of it — four whole points — says a bad half-hour with a stranger is very recoverable.

03 — How good were these conversations

Nearly everybody enjoyed it

Asked how enjoyable the conversation was, 78% of participants answered 7 or higher on a 1–9 scale and 28.6% gave the maximum of 9. Only 2.4% scored 3 or below. This is a ceiling-heavy measure, which matters for everything that follows.

The three things that predict a good conversation with a stranger — liking them, thinking they are a good conversationalist, and believing they like you — are all about the relationship. Not one of them is about who the two people are.
04 — The central finding

You liked them more than you thought they liked you. They felt the same way.

Each participant answered two questions on a 1–7 scale: how much they like their partner, and how much they think their partner likes them. The first averages 6.00. The second averages 5.35. Both people in a pair make this error at the same time.

Why this is not a scale artefact

Both questions use the same 1–7 scale and were answered by the same people in the same minute, so the gap cannot come from different anchors. It is symmetric across the pair: the mean of what partners actually report (6.00) equals the mean of what people report feeling (6.00), while the mean of what people guess sits 0.65 points lower.

The asymmetry is the story. Strangers are generous in what they feel and pessimistic in what they believe is felt back.

After thirty minutes of talking, you still do not know whether the person across from you likes you — and the safe bet is that they like you more than you think.
05 — Reading each other

Thirty minutes buys you almost no insight

Because every conversation contains two people rating the same event, we can check guesses against ground truth. The checks are unflattering. Guesses about being liked correlate r = 0.19 with the partner's actual liking; actual mutual liking correlates only r = 0.19; and the two partners' ratings of how enjoyable the same conversation was correlate just r = 0.18.

Disagreement is the norm, not the exception

Take one partner's enjoyment rating and subtract the other's. The average absolute gap is 1.43 points on a 9-point scale, the median is 1, and in the most extreme pair the two people rated the identical conversation 8 points apart.

Two people can leave the same half-hour with genuinely different memories of it. Any summary that averages a pair's ratings — as convos.csv does throughout — is blending two experiences that were only loosely connected.

What does line up

The weak correlations are not an artefact of everyone scoring high. Enjoyment has a wide spread at pair level, and shared_reality — the sense that you and your partner see things the same way — varies from 1 to 7 across people.

It is specifically the cross-person signal that is faint. You know how you felt. What you cannot recover, from inside the conversation, is how the other person felt.

Loneliness, judged from the outside

Participants also rated their own loneliness and their partner's, on a 1–3 scale. Among the 1,143 people who answered both, own loneliness averages 1.80 and the partner's 1.53: we see ourselves as lonelier than the stranger on screen. And the two ratings matter differently. Thinking your partner is lonely goes with a noticeably worse conversation (r = −0.22 with enjoyment), while your own loneliness barely registers (r = −0.06).

06 — Systematic biases

Everyone thinks they are the one contributing less

Several items come in matched pairs: what you think of yourself, what you think your partner thinks of you, and what you think of your partner. Line them up and a consistent bias appears — the self is rated below the partner, and the partner's opinion of the self is rated below the opinion actually given.

Turn length, status, intelligence, kindness, loneliness, liking: six separate measures, one direction. The self comes out lower and the partner comes out higher.
07 — Differences between pairs

Who you are matched with matters far less than you would guess

The pair file records how similar or distant the two people were: same sex, same race, years apart in age, points apart in politics. Almost none of it moves the needle on enjoyment. What does move it is whether the two people experienced the conversation the same way.

Being similar on paper buys you almost nothing. Feeling that you and the other person are seeing the same thing buys you a full point of enjoyment.
08 — Summary

Six things this dataset says

1. Talking to a stranger reliably improves your mood. Mean affect goes from 6.16 at the beginning to 7.38 at the end, a gain of 1.23 points; 2,153 of 3,257 people ended happier than they began, against 248 who ended worse. Arousal rises in step, from 5.67 to 6.70.

2. The gain is largest for people who need it most. Starting mood of 1 predicts a rise of 4.00 points; starting mood of 9 predicts a fall of 0.21.

3. These conversations are almost universally rated enjoyable — 78% of ratings are 7 or above on a 9-point scale, 28.6% are the maximum. The measure is ceiling-heavy, so differences between groups are compressed by design.

4. People systematically under-estimate how much they are liked. Own liking averages 6.00 of 7, guessed liking 5.35, and the partner's actual liking 6.00. 1,691 people fall on the pessimistic side of that gap and only 154 on the optimistic side. The same discount appears for status (5.53 versus 5.91), intelligence (7.06 versus 7.68) and kindness (7.43 versus 7.98).

5. Partners cannot read each other. Guesses about being liked correlate r = 0.19 with the truth; mutual liking correlates r = 0.19; the two people's enjoyment ratings for the same conversation correlate r = 0.18 and differ by 1.43 points on average, up to 8. Each side also believes they did more of the talking (5.40 versus 5.03).

6. Demographics are a weak lever; shared experience is a strong one. Same-sex pairs (7.493) and different-sex pairs (7.488) enjoy conversations identically, and same-race pairs (7.42) rate them slightly lower than mixed-race pairs (7.55). Political distance costs up to 0.4 points and an age gap costs common ground (6.18 → 5.27) but not enjoyment. Disagreement about shared reality costs over a point (7.52 → 6.44).

Caveats a reader should keep in mind

  • Missingness is informative. The 55 rows with no post-conversation ratings are people who dropped out mid-call. Everything about change over time describes only those who stayed.
  • Ceiling effects. With 78% of enjoyment ratings at 7 or above, group differences are squeezed into the top of the scale. A 0.1-point difference here is not obviously meaningful.
  • Correlations, not causes. Every association reported here is cross-sectional within a single conversation. Liking and enjoyment being correlated at 0.87 says nothing about which drives which.
  • Repeat participants. 754 of the 1,456 people appear in more than one conversation, so person-level means are not independent observations.
  • The politics scale has a hole. Value 5 never occurs, so politics_diff cannot be a clean count of scale steps; it is used here only as a relative distance.
  • Self-report only. Turn length, loneliness and shared reality are all perceptions. There is no behavioural measure in these files against which to check them.
